\begin{abstract}
We study whether a learner's prior learning history changes how it will learn in the future, and which internal state carries this effect. Our Developmental Learning Architecture (DLA) keeps standard MLP/Transformer backbones unchanged but augments every weight with per-parameter fast weights, plasticity, and sleep consolidation, forming a fast/slow learner state. Using a 6.59M Chinese character-level GPT we show: (i) DLA matches tuned AdamW on new-domain adaptation while keeping consolidated slow memory essentially immune to forgetting; (ii) different curriculum histories lead to different future adaptation on an unseen domain; (iii) this history-dependent future adaptation is causally tied to the fast-weight state $\Wfast$ --- replacing the hard$\to$easy learner's fast weights with those from an easy$\to$hard learner significantly degrades future adaptation ($n{=}12$, gain@40 difference $-0.019$, bootstrap CI excludes zero, Cohen's $d\approx-0.7$); (iv) norm-matching and within-matrix shuffling do not remove the effect; (v) localization is suggestive of MLP and attention contributions; (vi) fair baselines (AdamW, replay, EWC) also show history sensitivity, but DLA is most robust after a difficult history; and (vii) the destructive $\Wfast$ effect replicates on a second Shakespeare character-level backbone. We do \emph{not} find evidence that more experience monotonically accelerates learning. The paper therefore proposes a precise framing: developmental change does not equal universally faster learning; rather, learning history leaves a persistent, partially localizable trace in transient learner state, with $\Wfast$ providing a causal component of history-dependent future adaptation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Standard neural training assumes the learner is a fixed function plus an external optimizer. This paper asks a more developmental question: can the way a model learns change as a result of what it has learned? We build a minimal architecture, DLA, in which each weight matrix is augmented by fast weights, plasticity, and sleep consolidation. The backbone is not modified.
Our empirical questions are: (1) Does learning history change future adaptation on a new task? (2) Which internal state carries that effect? (3) Does the effect manifest as faster learning? We find support for the first two and a clear negative result for the third.

\section{Method}
For every 2D weight matrix we maintain $\Wslow$, $\Wfast$, plasticity $P$, and eligibility $Q$:
\begin{equation}
  W_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Wslow + \mathrm{softplus}(P) \odot \Wfast.
\end{equation}
The online update uses Adam-style moments on $\Wfast$ modulated by $\mathrm{softplus}(P)$; $P$ is updated by a meta-plasticity rule. At task boundaries (sleep), $\Wslow$ is updated from $Q$ and a small direct transfer of $\Wfast$, and $\Wfast$ is decayed.
To create histories we order three curriculum domains easy$\to$hard (EH) or hard$\to$easy (HE) and then probe an unseen domain $D$ under a fixed budget.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Primary backbone: 6.59M Chinese character GPT (6 layers, 8 heads, 256 dim, vocab 7280). Curriculum: Wikipedia, SFT-style QA, science Wikipedia. Unseen D: science slice. Second setting: $\sim$10.65M Shakespeare character GPT. Baselines: AdamW, AdamW$+$replay, EWC.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Fast/slow separation protects consolidated memory}
Stage 4 formal (5 seeds): DLA matches tuned AdamW on adaptation (gain $+14.0\%$ vs $+13.0\%$) while consolidated slow-memory forgetting is $\approx 0$ ($-0.4\%\pm0.2$).

\subsection{Learning history changes future adaptation}
Hard$\to$easy history leads to better future adaptation on unseen $D$ than easy$\to$hard. This effect is not unique to DLA, but DLA is most robust after a difficult history.

\subsection{Scalar learning-rule parameters are not a stable carrier}
Body $\times$ $\phi$ cross-injection shows body replacement changes future adaptation much more than replacing the 9-dimensional tempo vector. $\phi$ is not a stable/dominant carrier in this setting.

\subsection{$\Wfast$ is a causal component}
Core causal result ($n{=}12$): HE/HE gain@40 $+0.014$; HE$+$EH $\Wfast$ $-0.005$. Paired contrast: mean $-0.019$, 95\% CI $[-0.033,-0.005]$, $d\approx-0.71$, sign p$=0.019$ (10/12 negative). Initial PPL differences are small; the effect appears in the adaptation trajectory.

\subsection{Second-setting replication}
Shakespeare char GPT, 2 seeds: HE/HE gain@40 $+0.052$, $+0.042$; HE$+$EH $\Wfast$ $+0.004$, $-0.001$. Direction replicates.

\subsection{Negative result: development $\neq$ faster learning}
Stage 5 difficulty-normalized curriculum: flat LE. Stage 6 matched-difficulty: p$=0.17$. Stage 7 cross-domain 20 seeds: p$=0.82$. Stage 8 physics near-transfer 20 seeds: $d=-0.17$. We do not claim faster learning.

\section{Discussion}
Safe claims: fast/slow separation; history changes future adaptation; $\Wfast$ is a causal component; norm/shuffle do not remove the effect; no evidence for monotonic acceleration.
Suggestive: exact module localization; content vs dynamics; second-setting n is small.
Must not claim: $\Wfast$ is the only carrier; $\phi$ has no effect; development means universally faster learning; sleep consolidation strongly writes into $\Wslow$; small-model results transfer to LLMs.

\section{Conclusion}
A learner is not fully characterized by what it currently knows. Learning history leaves a persistent, partially localizable trace in transient learner state, with $\Wfast$ providing a causal component of history-dependent future adaptation. We do not find evidence that this developmental effect simply manifests as faster learning.
